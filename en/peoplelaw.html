One legal change never ends with one fix

01 · The problem in practice

When one external regulation changes, as with Korea's 52-hour workweek, the way working hours are entered, the payroll process, the payroll system's calculation logic, and the labor-management negotiation all have to change with it.

This repeats whenever a statute or court ruling changes. Yet many companies have no one dedicated to HR compliance, so an HR practitioner handles it alongside other duties, and the record of what was affected and why ends up scattered across one person's files and memory. When that person moves on, the next change starts the same review from scratch. PeopleLaw's core goal was to map that scattered context inside the company, so the work continues without gaps on the same context and history even when the person in charge or the users change.

The gap existing solutions leave open

02 · The problem, seen again in the MBA

In the MBA I looked at this problem again from the market's side.

  • Global HR SaaS is strong at HR operations, but connecting a legal change to each company's own policies and forms, which differ by country, industry, and company, stays with the customer.
  • A custom build by a local systems integrator can fit one company, but it has to be reworked whenever the law or an internal document changes.
  • A general-purpose AI can answer questions about pasted policies, but updating relationships when a source changes, separating what has not been confirmed, and handing the basis to the next person are still left undone.

What was missing was a layer that connects external change to the work inside a company and keeps that company's own context.

Turning an HR practitioner's judgment into product principles

03 · What I decided to build

PeopleLaw handles one legal change in four stages: detect, company-specific impact mapping, review and decide, execute and retain. The design was driven by the risks I had seen in HR practice, not by the technology.

What it gives the organization

  • Clear ownership of decisions. Who decided what, and on what basis, stays in one place, so no one has to gather scattered files again for a report or an audit.
  • People's time goes to judgment. The system and AI handle searching, comparing, and deadline calculation, so the practitioner can focus on applicability, wording, and procedure.
  • Individual experience becomes an organizational asset. Even after the person in charge changes, the next review starts from the links already confirmed and the reasons behind each decision. Past conclusions are not applied automatically. Changed conditions are checked again.

Inside the product, and in building it

04 · How I used AI

Inside the product, AI is given only clause candidates, before-and-after comparisons, and draft wording. A rule engine checks conditions and computes deadlines from reference values approved by a person, and the server checks the AI's citations and numbers against the official statute text. Anything that cannot be confirmed in the source stays "unconfirmed".

In building it, I decided what to build, the lines AI must not cross, and the approval criteria. The work was split across lead, core, and UI sessions; a model from a different provider than the one that wrote the code reviewed it read-only; and only changes that passed the required tests were merged. Core data structures and reference values for judgment cannot change without my approval record, and a change without one fails the automated checks. Each design decision is written down in its own document.

What the cross-review found

In the first cross-review, the other model found that confirming a clause's impact and approving its wording shared a single status value. A practitioner who only confirmed the impact could have let AI-drafted wording enter an internal notice as if it were confirmed. In HR practice these are two different approvals, so I separated them and added a record of who confirmed the wording and when.
