Case Study 01 · Product

PeopleLawAn AI-based HR compliance platform that maps regulatory changes to company-specific actions and records the decisions behind them

I built PeopleLaw to solve a problem I lived with over 12 years in HR. When a labor law or a company condition changes, PeopleLaw identifies the policy clauses, form fields, and HR information system (HRIS) settings that may need to change, and keeps a record of who confirmed what, on which basis.

One external change never ends with one internal edit

This started from a problem I met repeatedly over 12 years in HR. When a statute or a court ruling changes, fixing one internal policy is not the end of it. The HR practitioner works out by hand how far the change reaches inside the company.

Policies

Which clause?

Work rules (취업규칙, the binding employment rules a Korean employer must maintain) and internal policies.

Forms

Which field?

Employment contracts, request forms, consent forms.

Systems and procedure

Which setting, in what order?

Payroll and attendance settings, then internal notice, employee consultation, and filing, each with its own evidence.

The scope and depth of the impact vary from case to case, and it spreads across policies, contracts, payroll and attendance systems, and labor-management procedure. Yet many companies have no one dedicated solely to HR compliance or labor relations, so an HR practitioner handles this alongside other duties.

As a result, the history and context of what was affected and why a decision was made end up scattered across one person's files and memory. When that person moves on, the history is not handed over in any systematic way, and the next change starts the same review from scratch.

Why existing solutions leave this open

Global HR SaaS

Strong at HR operations, but fitting each company's differences stays with the customer

Products such as Workday and SAP SuccessFactors serve customers in many countries. Labor law differs by country, the applicable criteria differ by industry, size, and workforce mix, and every company uses its own policies, forms, and systems. So when a law changes, connecting it to this company's clauses and forms remains the customer's job.

Custom build by a local systems integrator

It can be built for one company, but it must be reworked at every change

A custom build is designed from scratch for each company. It has to be revised whenever the law or an internal document changes, and that maintenance burden stays with the company.

Between external change and internal work, a layer that understands and maintains one company's own context was missing. PeopleLaw builds that layer.

Structure and design intent

PeopleLaw processes one legal change to the end in four stages. It collects official statutes daily and compares the text before and after the amendment (detect), then checks the legal duty against company facts to find the policy clauses, form fields, and system settings affected (company-specific impact mapping). Next it settles the revised wording and procedure (review and decide), and keeps the fulfillment, evidence, and decision history for reuse at the next change (execute and retain).

Each stage has a clear owner. AI drafts, the rule engine supplies the basis for judgment, and people make the final decision. Legal judgment is never auto-confirmed. It goes to an expert for review.

Why it is designed this way: an HR practitioner's view

01 · Impact mapping at the center

It does not stop at an alert

Knowing that a law changed is different from the company actually changing. In practice the effort went into finding which policy clause, form field, and HRIS setting to touch, not into reading the amendment. So company-specific impact mapping is the core of the product.

02 · Three responsibilities

AI drafts, rules supply the basis, people decide

The wording of work rules becomes an employee's working conditions. So AI output is stored only as a draft, and the practitioner and the approver confirm applicability and wording. The rule engine checks conditions and computes deadlines from reference values approved by a person. With AI off, the same flow still completes.

03 · Legal safety line

The system does not make final legal determinations

Whether a revision is a disadvantageous change to work rules determines which procedure the company must follow. Such questions always stay "needs review" and go to an expert. There are five areas: disadvantageous change to work rules, justification of dismissal or discipline, employer status and collective bargaining, occupational safety violations, and the legality of automated hiring and evaluation decisions.

04 · Keeping the basis

The basis outlives the person in charge

Who decided, when, on what basis, and which evidence confirmed fulfillment are kept together per case, so the next change and the next handover can start from that record.

FAQ

Does the AI make legal judgments?

No. AI produces drafts only: clause mapping, comparison, wording. The rule engine checks conditions and computes procedures. People confirm applicability and wording. In the five restricted areas the system routes the question to an expert.

Why not paste the company policies into a general-purpose AI and ask?

That is a real alternative. PeopleLaw builds in the parts around the answer: updating relationships when a source changes, separating what has not been confirmed, and handing the basis to the next person. I think the fair comparison is the total effort to keep the same level of review and record.

You did not write the code yourself. How did you verify it?

AI agents wrote the code. I decided what to build and what to approve. I checked development results with a defined set of automated tests, cross-review by an AI model from a different provider, and human approval. These checks do not guarantee quality beyond what they cover.
